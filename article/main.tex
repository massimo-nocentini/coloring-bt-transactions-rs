\begin{abstract}
Every Bitcoin transaction inherits its coins from earlier ones, and so from the
blocks that minted them. The \emph{colour} of a transaction is the distribution
over block ids that its value descends from. This article describes a program
that computes it: what the colouring rule is, why it is a triangular solve, the
representations a colour can take, the vector kernels and threading that make it
fast, and --- the part that decides everything else --- why the exact colour of
every transaction in a 778-million-record chain cannot be materialised on any
machine we have, and what can be produced instead. Along the way $A$ itself is
written down: 150\,GB of records hold 10.6\,GB of matrix, and writing it once
means no later run parses the chain again.

All figures are measurements on the 2022 chain
(\texttt{finalBCUTXO\_2022.scm}, $149\,968\,404\,213$ bytes, $778\,613\,438$
transactions) on a 112-thread Xeon Gold 6238R with 503\,GB of memory. Anything
extrapolated is labelled as such.
\end{abstract}

\section{The rule}
\label{sec:rule}

Transaction records arrive in chain order. Each names its block, its own id, the
transactions its inputs spend and for how much, and its outputs. The colouring
rule is two lines:

\begin{itemize}
  \item a \emph{coinbase} transaction --- one with no inputs --- is coloured by
        the block that minted it, $x^{b}$;
  \item any other transaction is the weighted sum of the colours of the
        transactions its inputs spend, each weighted by the fraction of this
        transaction's value that came through it.
\end{itemize}

Writing $a_i$ for the amount of input $i$ and $C_i$ for the colour of the
transaction it spends,
\begin{equation}
  C \;=\; \sum_i \frac{a_i}{\sum_j a_j}\, C_i .
  \label{eq:rule}
\end{equation}

Two facts follow immediately and are worth stating because they are what make
the implementation checkable. First, every colour is a probability distribution
over block ids: the weights of \eqref{eq:rule} sum to one by construction and
each $C_i$ sums to one by induction. The program tracks the worst drift from
that invariant and reports it under \flag{stats}; over the whole 2022 chain it
is $1.02\times10^{-13}$. Second, the \emph{support} --- which blocks appear ---
does not depend on the weights at all, so the unweighted and weighted backends
compute the same set of blocks and differ only in the coefficients.

The unweighted reading is older and simpler: give every input weight one, and a
colour becomes a \emph{set} of block ids, with $\vee$ for combination. That is
the form the original Scheme program computed, and the program keeps it
(\flag{rings}, \flag{sets}) because two implementations that share no code and
produce byte-identical output are a much stronger statement about the fast one
than any test it could be given alone.

\section{It is a triangular solve}

Index the transactions in chain order and let $A$ be the matrix with
$A_{ji}$ equal to the fraction of transaction $j$'s value spent from
transaction~$i$. Every spend refers to an earlier record, so

\begin{quote}
  \textbf{$A$ is strictly lower triangular.}
\end{quote}

Let $C$ be the coinbase matrix, with $C_{ib}=1$ when transaction $i$ is the
coinbase of block $b$, and let $B$ be the colour matrix we want, one row per
transaction and one column per block. Rule~\eqref{eq:rule} says
\begin{equation}
  B \;=\; AB + C
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  (I-A)\,B \;=\; C
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  B \;=\; (I-A)^{-1} C .
  \label{eq:solve}
\end{equation}

$I-A$ is unit lower triangular. The program's single streaming pass in chain
order \emph{is} forward substitution on it --- not an analogy, the same
arithmetic, row by row. This framing pays for itself three times over.

\paragraph{It explains the cost.} $(I-A)^{-1} = I + A + A^2 + \cdots$ is the
weighted transitive closure, and the classical fact about triangular matrices is
that the inverse of a sparse one is dense: it fills in along reachability. That
fill-in \emph{is} the memory and output problem of
Section~\ref{sec:wall}. No reordering helps, because the row order is fixed by
the chain and we are solving, not factoring.

\paragraph{It names the working set.} The rows a forward substitution must keep
are those still referenced by later rows --- exactly the transactions with an
output that will be spent again.

\paragraph{It says what \emph{can} be computed.} The solve is linear, so any
linear functional of the colour commutes with it. For any $n_{\text{blocks}}
\times L$ matrix $P$,
\begin{equation}
  BP \;=\; (I-A)^{-1}(CP).
  \label{eq:project}
\end{equation}
The projected colours come from running the \emph{same} forward substitution
against an $L$-column right-hand side instead of a $762\,261$-column one. Memory
per transaction becomes $L$ numbers, flat in colour size, and the result is
exact for whatever $P$ projects onto. Section~\ref{sec:projections} is the
catalogue of useful $P$.

Storing $A$ itself is cheap and worth mentioning: at $2\,044\,897\,328$
nonzeros, a CSR of $A$ is about 19.5\,GB (row pointers 3.1, column indices 8.2,
values 8.2), against a 150\,GB text file that parses at roughly 200\,MB/s. Since
column indices satisfy $j<i$, storing the back-distance $i-j$ makes them small
--- 53--56\% of spends reach back under $10^5$ records --- and a varint encoding
takes that 8.2\,GB to 2--3\,GB. Parse once, solve many.

\section{Representations of a colour}
\label{sec:representations}

The program carries four, selected on the command line and driven through one
generic loop (\texttt{ColorStore} in \texttt{src/store.rs}).

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Flag & Representation & Bytes/term & Answers \\
\midrule
\flag{rings}    & circular linked lists   & 24 & which blocks \\
\flag{sets}     & \texttt{Rc<[u32]>} sorted & 4 & which blocks \\
\flag{weighted} & \texttt{u32} keys, \texttt{f64} weights & 12 & how much from each \\
\flag{bands} $K$ & $K$ \texttt{f32} lanes + 3 \texttt{f64} & $4K$/colour & how much, at band resolution \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The four backends. The first three are exact and grow with the colour;
the fourth is a projection and does not.}
\label{tab:backends}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Rings.} A port of a Knuth exercise: a colour is a circular list of
nodes, and combination walks two chains. It is kept as the reference. Its merge
loads \texttt{next} to find out where to load next, so it is latency-bound and
no vector instruction shortens a dependent load.

\paragraph{Sorted sets.} A colour is \texttt{Rc<[u32]>}, block ids ascending. A
union is two sequential reads and one sequential write, which the prefetcher
handles. Three shortcuts precede the merge: two handles on one allocation, an
empty side, and --- common in this workload --- two ranges that do not overlap,
which makes the union a concatenation.

\paragraph{Weighted.} Blocks and weights in \emph{two} arrays rather than one
array of pairs. Interleaving would be one allocation instead of three, but it
would put a block id between every two weights, and the arithmetic runs over
weights alone. Keeping them contiguous is what lets the vector kernels of
Section~\ref{sec:simd} work.

\paragraph{Bands.} Section~\ref{sec:projections}.

\section{Vector kernels}
\label{sec:simd}

Four pieces of the program are shaped like vector work, and they are the only
four. Everything else was measured and left scalar.

\subsection{Finding the end of a run of digits}

The record reader walks 150\,GB one byte at a time, and the thing it does to
nearly every byte is ask how long the run of digits starting here is. A byte is
a digit exactly when $b - \texttt{'0'}$ is at most 9 as an \emph{unsigned} byte
--- one subtract, one compare, no range pair.

On \textsc{aarch64} the compare gives 16 lanes of \texttt{0x00}/\texttt{0xFF};
there is no \texttt{movemask}, so the standard stand-in is \texttt{vshrn} by 4,
narrowing the 16 lanes into 16 nibbles of one \texttt{u64}. The first non-digit
is at nibble $\texttt{trailing\_zeros}/4$. On \textsc{x86-64}, SSE2 compares are
signed only, so the subtracted byte is biased by \texttt{0x80} first
($x \oplus \texttt{0x80} < 10 \oplus \texttt{0x80}$ signed is $x<10$ unsigned),
and \texttt{movemask} hands back the lane results directly.

Both are baseline for their target, so there is no runtime feature detection and
no dispatch. Every vector path has a scalar twin and the tests assert they agree
across alignments, across lengths either side of the 16-byte stride, and on
random bytes.

\subsection{Eight digits at a time}

Turning eight ASCII digits into a number is done with \textsc{swar} --- ``SIMD
within a register'' --- rather than a vector unit, because the answer has to end
up in a scalar register anyway. Three rounds of divide-and-conquer, each folding
neighbouring lanes into lanes twice as wide:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llr}
\toprule
lanes & multiplier & meaning \\
\midrule
$8\times$\,u8  & $2561$            & $10 \ll 8 \mid 1$ \\
$4\times$\,u16 & $6\,553\,601$     & $100 \ll 16 \mid 1$ \\
$2\times$\,u32 & $42\,949\,672\,960\,001$ & $10^4 \ll 32 \mid 1$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

One multiply does the whole $\text{hi}\cdot10^k+\text{lo}$ for every pair at
once. No lane can carry into its neighbour: the largest intermediate any round
produces is $99$, $9999$, $99\,999\,999$, each inside its lane. Overflow is then
checked once per eight digits rather than once per digit.

\subsection{Scaling weights}

The weighted merge finds \emph{runs} rather than stepping block by block,
because the longer the runs the more of the work lands in two flat loops:
\begin{align*}
  \texttt{scale\_into}    &: \quad d_k = s_k \cdot f \\
  \texttt{scale\_add\_into} &: \quad d_k = a_k f_a + b_k f_b
\end{align*}
These are left to the compiler rather than written by hand. A flat elementwise
multiply over two non-overlapping slices is exactly the shape LLVM's loop
vectoriser is built for: \texttt{zip} gives it the trip count up front and rules
out aliasing. That the runs are long is not an assumption --- counted over
records 1.4--1.5\,M of the 2022 chain, 97\% of the terms a merge writes arrive in
runs, the one-sided ones averaging 1900 blocks and the shared ones 140.

A hand-written vector \emph{merge} was tried and lost. Deciding which of two
sorted key arrays advances is a data-dependent branch per element, and the
branchless scalar version was faster.

\subsection{The band combine}

Under \flag{bands} there is no merge at all: two colours are the same length, so
combination is a $K$-lane \texttt{axpy}, $d_k = a_k w_a + b_k w_b$, which
vectorises without having to find runs first. This is the one kernel that got
\emph{better} by changing the representation rather than by tuning the code.

\subsection{Guarding it}

Nothing in the source says the compiler must vectorise, so nothing but the
disassembly can confirm it did. \texttt{make asm-check} disassembles the release
binary and counts vector multiplies, but only \emph{inside the symbols the scale
loops inline into}. Counting binary-wide fails open: on one build only 10 of 17
vector multiplies were the scale loops --- 6 were in \texttt{main} and 1 in an
unrelated closure --- so de-vectorising both loops entirely still left seven and
the check passed. Wrapping the loop bodies in \texttt{black\_box} took the count
inside \texttt{combine} from 10 to 0 and the old check still exited~0. The
per-symbol version fails, as it should.

\section{Threading}
\label{sec:threading}

\subsection{What is parallel and what is not}

The fold is a data-dependent walk of a DAG: transaction $N$ may spend
transaction $N-1$, so records cannot in general be coloured out of order. That
is the serial core. What \emph{is} parallel is everything around it.

Formatting turned out to be most of a text run. Holding the fold and the parse
constant by comparing against \flag{sum}, which walks exactly the same terms and
prints one number instead of all of them, over 150\,000 records:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrl}
\toprule
run & time & \\
\midrule
\flag{sets} \flag{fold} \texttt{/dev/null} & 2.43\,s & parse and fold, no lines \\
\flag{sets}                                & 4.70\,s & $+$ 2.3\,GB of terms \\
\flag{weighted} \flag{sum}                 & 3.37\,s & parse and weighted fold \\
\flag{weighted}                            & 50.18\,s & $+$ 9.9\,GB of terms \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

So formatting is 47\% of an unweighted text run and 94\% of a weighted one. The
gap is the shortest round-tripping decimal a weighted coefficient prints as:
about 133\,ns a term against 15\,ns for the integer path.

\subsection{The formatter pool}

\flag{threads} $n$ spends $n$ threads formatting and one more reading records,
leaving the main thread the fold.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
& serial & threaded & the fold alone \\
\midrule
\flag{weighted} & 50.44\,s & \textbf{5.22\,s} (16 threads) & 3.37\,s \\
\flag{sets}     & 4.67\,s  & \textbf{1.67\,s} (8 threads)  & \\
\flag{rings}    & 11.16\,s & \textbf{6.48\,s} (8 threads)  & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two design points carry this.

\paragraph{A copy crosses the boundary, not a handle.} Sharing the colour would
mean \texttt{Rc} becoming \texttt{Arc}, atomics on a refcount the fold thread
touches several times a record, and --- the real cost --- an ownership question
that has no race-free answer once two threads can hold a handle: the stores
decide whether an allocation is gone by asking whether they hold the last one.
Copying sidesteps all of it. A term is 12 bytes and a formatted term about 22,
so the copy is a memcpy against a decimal conversion: roughly 1\,ns a term
against 133. The backends keep their \texttt{Rc} and their layouts, and the ring
arena --- which could not have been shared at all --- gets the same speedup.

\paragraph{Ordering is free.} Record $i$ goes to worker $i \bmod n$ and the
writer collects from worker $i \bmod n$. No sequence numbers, no reorder buffer,
and the output is byte-identical to serial at every width --- which matters
beyond tidiness, since diffing \flag{rings} against \flag{sets} is how the fast
backend is checked.

Dispatch is \emph{batched}, 256 records or 8192 terms whichever comes first.
Per-record dispatch made a threaded run \emph{slower than serial} on records
whose colours stay small: a microsecond of channel to hand over a line worth a
hundred nanoseconds of work. Measured over a million single-block colours, where
serial is 0.58\,s: per-record dispatch gives 1.59/0.89/1.19/1.36\,s at
1/2/4/8~threads; batched gives 0.43\,s at all of them.

\subsection{Closing the pipeline is not optional}

\texttt{Pool::finish} drains and reports, and the driver calls it --- but only on
the path that reaches the end of the records. Every early return skipped it, and
dropping a \texttt{JoinHandle} \emph{detaches} the thread rather than waiting, so
the process could exit with up to a megabyte still in the writer's buffer.
Measured on 200\,000 records with one bad record at the end: serial wrote
$2\,088\,890$ bytes and \flag{threads}~4 wrote about $1\,250\,000$ of them, a
byte-exact prefix ending mid-line, varying run to run, with the same exit code
and the same message on stderr. On a short enough file it wrote nothing at all.
The closing now lives in \texttt{Drop}, where the language runs it, and
\texttt{finish} is the way to get at the \emph{error} rather than the way to make
the output happen.

\subsection{Where threading does not help}

\flag{sum} and \flag{moments} refuse \flag{threads}, measured rather than
assumed: a collapsed line is a few numbers, and copying the terms to a worker
costs more than collapsing them in place. Over 150\,000 records \flag{sum} goes
from 3.34\,s serial to 5.20/4.54/4.51\,s at 2/4/8 threads, and \flag{moments}
from 3.58\,s to 3.95/4.18/4.22\,s.

Level-parallel folding is worse than useless for the exact fold. The set of live
colours at any chain prefix is a property of the DAG and not of evaluation
order, so a level-parallel fold that computes each colour once has peak memory
at least the serial peak; measured, the ratio is $1.497$ on an early window.
Against a binding memory wall that is a net loss. It is the right tool for the
\emph{binned} fold, where memory is bounded --- the real chain's mean level width
in a $10^5$-record window is 44.2 early and 458.2 deep, so 16--64 threads stay
busy on 97\% of the chain.

\section{The allocator is not a detail}
\label{sec:allocator}

At depth the fold is not merge-bound, it is \texttt{malloc}-bound. The
allocation pattern --- hundreds of thousands of live blocks between 50\,KB and
1\,MB, freed and reallocated at every merge --- is the one glibc's allocator
handles worst: measured over records 1.4--1.5\,M, a \texttt{malloc} costs
26\,\textmu s against 47\,ns at the head of the chain, a quarter of the fold's
time.

Two changes that only work together: jemalloc as the global allocator (behind a
default feature, with \texttt{-{}-no-default-features} giving glibc back), and a
merge that writes into a fresh uninitialised allocation at the upper bound and
shrinks it to the length the merge turned out to have, instead of zero-filling a
staging buffer and copying out. The shrink is a downward \texttt{realloc}, which
jemalloc does in place and to size and glibc does not.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
1\,500\,000 records, \flag{weighted} \flag{sum} & time & resident \\
\midrule
glibc $+$ staged merge      & 257.71\,s & 30.61\,GiB \\
jemalloc $+$ in-place merge & \textbf{148.47\,s} & \textbf{30.48\,GiB} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

$1.74\times$ faster with memory 0.4\% lower, output md5-identical. At 800\,000
records the same pair is 60.28\,s/9.15\,GiB against 38.39\,s/9.59\,GiB --- faster
but 4.8\% \emph{more} resident, so the memory saving is a property of depth
rather than of the change. Depth is where the wall is.

\section{The wall}
\label{sec:wall}

\subsection{It is a census, not an extrapolation}

The driver holds a colour for every transaction with an unspent output. A
colour-free pass over the whole file counts them: $92\,735\,490$ at the end of
the 2022 chain and $92\,753\,575$ at peak. A 450\,GB budget over that count is
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{450 \times 10^9}{92\,735\,490} \;=\; 4852 \text{ bytes a colour}
\end{equation*}
--- 404 weighted terms at 12 bytes, or 1213 set terms at 4. The mean colour at
$1\,500\,000$ records, which is 0.19\% of the file, is already 5984 blocks and
growing as $\text{records}^{1.37}$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrr}
\toprule
records & mean colour (blocks) & distinct terms & resident \\
\midrule
$500\,000$   & 1\,087  & 1.8\,GB & \\
$1\,000\,000$ & 4\,723  & 15.2\,GB & \\
$1\,500\,000$ & 5\,984  & 27.7\,GB & 32\,GB \\
$3\,000\,000$ & 12\,754 & 102.6\,GB & 107.75\,GiB \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The store is made of terms and not overhead: at 3\,M records, \texttt{mallinfo2}
reports 102.59\,GB in use against $\text{terms}\times12 = 102.57$\,GB, leaving
\texttt{Rc} headers and the hash map at 25\,MB, or 0.02\%. Throughput over that
span fell from 958\,k records/s to 760.

Live terms grow as roughly $\text{records}^{2}$ in the last window and the local
exponent is \emph{rising} (1.48, 1.45, 1.82, 2.28 over successive half-million
windows), so a single power law understates it. Fits that honour the rise put
the 450\,GiB wall between 4.8\,M and 8\,M records for the current code and
8--11\,M with every exact lever pulled --- in all cases under 1.5\% of the file.

\subsection{The conclusion}

\begin{quote}
  \textbf{The exact colour polynomial of every transaction in the 2022 chain
  cannot be materialised on this machine, under any per-transaction
  representation, and it is not close.}
\end{quote}

This is not an implementation failure. It is the fill-in of $(I-A)^{-1}$ in
\eqref{eq:solve}: the answer is a dense $778{,}613{,}438 \times 762{,}261$
object. Even freezing the colour size at the 12\,754 blocks measured at 0.4\% of
the chain, a materialised store is 12.9\,TiB weighted, 4.3\,TiB as sets, or
8.0\,TiB as one bit per block. Output is the same story: at about 22 bytes a
term the full text is over 100\,TB.

\section{Freeing colours earlier}
\label{sec:oracle}

``Has an unspent output'' is the only question a \emph{stream} can ask, and it is
the wrong one. What the fold needs is whether anything \emph{later in this file}
will read the colour again --- and $51\,815\,075$ of the 136 million outputs
unspent at the end of the chain are \texttt{OP\_RETURN}, provably unspendable and
untouched by any of the file's $2\,044\,897\,328$ inputs. (Subtracting them
leaves $84\,309\,568$, matching the publicly reported UTXO count at that height
to 0.34\% --- a pleasant confirmation that the census is measuring the right
thing.)

A file has a future, so the right question can be answered by reading the records
once first. \bin{examples/lastspend.rs} does that in one pass and writes one
\texttt{u32} per transaction: the record that spends it last, or \texttt{NEVER}.
Over the whole chain that is 5:27 wall, 2.9\,GB resident, and a 3.11\,GB file.
\flag{release-oracle} hands it to the fold.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
400\,000 records, \flag{weighted} \flag{sum} & time & resident & live terms at end \\
\midrule
plain  & 5.67\,s & 947\,MB & $67\,426\,186$ \\
oracle & \textbf{5.26\,s} & \textbf{258\,MB} & \textbf{0} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

$3.67\times$ less memory and slightly faster, with byte-identical output under
every backend. The peak over the whole chain drops from $92\,753\,575$ colours
to $19\,361\,261$, a factor of $4.79$.

Three things guard correctness, because a stale oracle would free a colour that
is still wanted and produce a quietly wrong answer: a magic string; a record
count checked before any record is read; and a per-record bound, checked
\emph{after} end-of-file is detected so that a run exactly matching its oracle
is not rejected on the iteration that finds EOF. The count check is an
inequality, not a match --- an oracle written for the whole file is safe on any
prefix of it, since it only claims a colour is read \emph{later} than the prefix
reaches, which makes the fold conservative.

One subtlety is worth recording. A record that spends the same transaction more
than once must free it on the \emph{lowest} input index that names it, because
the fold walks its inputs from the last to the first and the lowest index is the
one it reaches last. Freeing on the highest would drop the colour while inputs
below still wanted it.

\section{Writing $A$ down}
\label{sec:matrix}

Section~\ref{sec:oracle} spent one pass over the file to make every later run
cheaper. This is that trade taken as far as it goes.

The fold reads five numbers out of a record that averages 193 bytes: the block,
the transaction id, each input's amount and the transaction it spends, and how
many outputs there are. The reader skips the rest without converting its digits
--- and skips it again on every run. Every projection, every $K$, every change to
what is emitted rather than to what is folded, pays a re-parse of 150\,GB first.

$A$ is what those five numbers are. \bin{examples/matrix.rs} writes it in one
pass and \flag{matrix} reads it back.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrc}
\toprule
the 2022 chain as & bytes & vs.\ text & exact \\
\midrule
records, \texttt{finalBCUTXO\_2022.scm}   & 149\,968\,404\,213 & 1.0 & yes \\
\flag{csr}, \texttt{u32/u32/f32}          & 19\,473\,632\,380  & 7.7 & \textbf{no} \\
\flag{compact}                            & \textbf{10\,601\,433\,514} & \textbf{14.1} & yes \\
\flag{structure}, no amounts              & 6\,126\,089\,724 & 24.5 & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The compact file is written in 401.2\,s and read back against the records field
for field in 461.6\,s: 778\,613\,440 records, 2\,044\,897\,328 nonzeros, every
block id reconstructed from the coinbase rows alone, content hashes agreeing, in
8\,MB resident. That is \flag{check}, and it is the reason the rest of this
section is measurement rather than assertion.

\flag{verify} is the same question asked without the 150\,GB: a sweep of the
matrix alone, recomputing the header's content hash and every count, 108.6\,s
over the whole chain at 5\,MB resident. It exists because a fold does not check
one. Of 347\,416 single-bit flips inside the rows of a 20\,000-record matrix,
102\,220 are accepted by the reader's per-row invariants --- and a flip at byte
700\,007 of a 500\,000-record matrix folds to exit 0, an empty stderr and a
different answer. The row checks catch a damaged \emph{shape}; only the hash
catches a damaged \emph{number}. The fold does not pay the 72\% the hash adds to
a sweep, and could not use it if it did: a hash does not settle until the last
row, where the fold's output has been streaming since the first.

\paragraph{The 19.5\,GB was the lossy one.} That figure is exactly
\texttt{u32 row\_ptr[n+1] + u32 col + f32 val}, and \flag{csr} still writes it,
because an argument about lossiness that is never run is only an argument. What
the run says: an \texttt{f32} weight has a worst relative error of
$5.96\times10^{-8}$ and only 28.10\% of the chain's weights round-trip at all;
the invariant of Section~\ref{sec:rule} degrades from $1.02\times10^{-13}$ to
$5.96\times10^{-8}$ before the fold composes anything; and replaying the fold on
those weights changes 44.71\% of \flag{sum} lines and 98.92\% of the coefficients
in the terms output. A colour is a distribution, and half the answers moving is
not a rounding difference.

The fix is not a wider float. A weight is $a_i / \sum_j a_j$ and both are
integers, so storing the raw \texttt{u64} satoshi amounts is exact --- and
smaller, 4.48 against 8.18\,GB, because amounts within a row are nothing like
uniformly distributed and a float is 32 bits of entropy by construction. The
exact file is $1.84\times$ \emph{smaller} than the lossy one. Nothing was bought
by the \texttt{f32}.

\paragraph{Where the bytes go.} One self-contained row per record, in record
order, one \texttt{u64} varint primitive throughout:

\midrule

Three of those rows are the whole argument against a CSR's fixed widths. A
\texttt{u32} row pointer costs 3.11\,GB to hold a number whose median is 1, where
a nibble and an escape cost 0.80; a \texttt{u32} column costs 8.18 where a delta
costs 5.32; and 73.14\% of rows have a single input, whose weight is exactly 1.0
on both branches of the share, so 569\,499\,647 amounts need not exist.

\paragraph{Two transforms measured and refused.} Sorting each row's parents would
shorten the delta chain by 0.89\,GB. It also changes the answer: the fold walks a
row's inputs from the last to the first, and reassociating an \texttt{f64} sum
moves 4.06\% of lines. Deduplicating a row that spends the same transaction twice
would save 0.13\,GB and free that colour at the wrong instant, for the reason
Section~\ref{sec:oracle} ends on. Both are reserved as flag bits a reader
refuses, so that neither is re-derived later and quietly believed.

\paragraph{One equivalence, again.} A run off the records and the same run off a
matrix are byte-identical, which is the only claim that matters and the crate's
usual way of making it. Checked at 50\,000 records for the three
pictures, a \flag{rows} window and a run under the release oracle; at 200\,000
for \flag{rings}, \flag{sets} \flag{threads}~8 and \flag{weighted}'s terms; at
500\,000 for \flag{rings}, \flag{sets} \flag{threads}~4, \flag{bands64}~128
\flag{moments}, \flag{weighted} \flag{moments} and \flag{bands}~4096
\flag{sum}; at 2\,000\,000 for both banded backends, \flag{weighted} \flag{sum}
and \flag{weighted} \flag{moments}; and at 20\,000\,000 for \flag{bands}~1024
\flag{moments}, 1\,149\,593\,609 bytes a side. The largest is a \flag{sets} pair
of 294.6\,GB at 2\,000\,000 records, byte for byte the same. \flag{rings} and
\flag{sets} still hash alike off a matrix, so the crate's oldest cross-check
survives the change of reader.

\paragraph{What it saves.} Less than the size ratio, and the honest number is the
smaller one. Over 20\,000\,000 records with the fold as cheap as it goes
(\flag{bands}~1 \flag{sum}) the run takes 17.8\,s off the text and 11.7\,s off the
matrix; at \flag{bands}~1024 \flag{moments}, where the fold is most of the work,
70.5\,s against 63.9\,s. Both are with the 150\,GB warm in a 503\,GB page cache,
which is the case that favours the text --- cold, the two artefacts are 12.5
minutes of reading against 50 seconds at the 205--270\,MB/s this array gives.
What the matrix buys is not a faster fold. It is that the parse is paid once.

\section{Projections: what can be computed}
\label{sec:projections}

Equation~\eqref{eq:project} is the whole story. Any fixed set of $L$ linear
functionals of the colour folds exactly in $L$ numbers per live transaction,
with no merge and no growth: the store stops depending on how many blocks a
colour names and depends only on how many colours are live. The exact polynomial
is $L = 762\,261$ functionals, which is exactly why it does not fit.

\subsection{Moments: $L=3$}

$P = [\,1,\; b,\; b^2\,]$ gives $\sum w$, $\sum wb$, $\sum wb^2$, from which the
mean block and the spread about it follow. \flag{moments} prints them.

\paragraph{Why three.} A projection is chosen rather than derived, so the choice
wants an argument. $L=1$ keeps only $\sum w$, which is $1$ by construction and so
says nothing about the colour --- though for exactly that reason its drift,
$1.02\times10^{-13}$ over the chain, audits the whole fold for free. $L=3$ is the
first size that answers the two questions one actually has of a distribution over
block ids: \emph{when} the coins came from, and \emph{how mixed} they are.
Nothing smaller reaches the second. Nothing larger is needed until the question
changes shape, which is what \flag{bands} is for. And because the functionals are
linear they ride the convex combination of \eqref{eq:rule} unchanged: the three
numbers the fold carries are the same three a materialised $762\,261$-term colour
would have been summarised into, not an estimate of them. A lossy view computed
exactly. That is what buys 47.6 bytes a record against $\sim$131\,648, and it is
the whole of what the pictures of Section~\ref{sec:pictures} consume --- mean to
hue, spread to chroma --- which is also why the mean has to be right on the block
axis and not merely nearby (Section~\ref{sec:carry}).

The effective block count, $\big(\sum w\big)^2/\sum w^2$, is \emph{quadratic} and
therefore outside the family: no bounded per-colour state folds it. That is the
precise boundary of what a projection can keep, and it is worth stating rather
than discovering.

\subsection{Bands: $L=K$}

$P$ = indicators of $K$ equal slices of block history. \flag{bands} $K$ answers
how much of a transaction's value came from each slice, the slices fixed against
the whole chain so a band means the same thing in every run.

and, crucially, \emph{flat with depth}. Projected over the census, \flag{bands}
256 holds 109\,GB and \flag{bands} 1024 holds 394\,GB unpruned; with the oracle
of Section~\ref{sec:oracle} the latter is 82\,GB.

\subsection{Carrying the moments alongside}
\label{sec:carry}

Deriving the moments from band indices would put a colour's mean at the
\emph{centre of a band} --- out by up to 372 blocks at $K=1024$ and 1488 at
$K=256$. For a number meant to read on the block axis that is not a rounding but
a wrong answer. So a colour carries $\sum w$, $\sum wb$, $\sum wb^2$ beside its
lanes, in \texttt{f64} whatever the lanes are. They are linear, so they ride the
same convex combination the lanes do. The cost is 5.6\% of time and 0.5\% of
memory; the mean then agrees with the exact fold to $1.1\times10^{-8}$ blocks at
every~$K$.

The hand-over was gated on \flag{moments} and should have been gated on the line
having a first moment to take. \flag{sum} is the same first moment and was not
given it, so \flag{bands}~$K$ \flag{sum} summed \emph{band centres} and nothing
refused it: a block-0 coinbase printed 372 at $K = 1024$ and 1\,488.5 at
$K = 256$, half a band each, and 372 blocks is the same error this subsection
exists to avoid. Both forms take the carried moments now, and a binned
\flag{sum} agrees with an exact \flag{weighted} \flag{sum} to
$6.1\times10^{-9}$ blocks over the first 200\,000 records at both $K$. It also
became faster --- 2.70\,s against 5.40\,s over 2\,000\,000 records --- because a
handed-over first moment does not walk the terms at all.

in taking the figure off a prefix. A band vector is only as sparse as the colour
is shallow, and at $K = 1024$ a mature colour reaches nearly every band --- 12
bytes a term times a thousand terms is the whole line, and 1\,211 bytes a record

2-million-line tail with \texttt{-T16}: \texttt{-3} gives 14.64\,B/record at
627\,MB/s, \texttt{-9} 12.42 at 151, \textbf{\texttt{-12} 12.28 at 66},

strongest that still consumes faster than the fold produces, so it is free.
\texttt{-19} would turn a twelve-minute run into two and a half hours for a
further 19\%. \texttt{-{}-long} earns nothing on this stream (503 bytes on a
4.7\,MB sample) because four short numbers repeat locally; it \emph{is} worth it
on the terms output, where fifty-kilobyte lines repeat across a window.

\section{Pictures}
\label{sec:pictures}

\subsection{One pixel per (transaction, block)}

\flag{png} draws a row per record and a column per block id, a pixel saying what
the colour says about that block: one bit under the unweighted backends, eight
under \flag{weighted}. \flag{pdf} and \flag{fold} fold that onto a bounded
canvas, shading each cell by how much of its rectangle is inked.

\flag{palette} reads the weighted samples through a colour ramp instead of as
greys. The image data is byte for byte what the greyscale run writes; the file
grows by 780 bytes, which is a \texttt{PLTE} chunk and its header. What it buys
is levels: grey has 254 and an eye reads perhaps thirty, and these weights live
in a fraction of a percent.

\subsection{Building a ramp that reads}

The ramp is built in Oklab, not HSL. HSL's ``lightness'' is not luminance and
its hue circle is wildly non-uniform --- yellow at $60^\circ$ and blue at
$240^\circ$ are nominally the same lightness and are nothing of the sort --- so a
ramp built in it has bands where the eye sees a step that is not there. For a
picture whose purpose is comparing magnitudes that is not cosmetic.

Lightness carries the magnitude, monotonically, which is what keeps the picture
readable photocopied, readable to a colour-deficient eye, and readable where it
is folded small enough that a cell is a sample rather than a shape. Hue and
chroma only separate levels that lightness leaves adjacent.

Most of the chroma one would like at the dark end is outside sRGB. Clamping the
channels there shifts the hue and flattens the sweep, so the gamut mapping holds
lightness and hue fixed and bisects the chroma down until the colour fits ---
256 colours computed once.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=1.2cm]{figures/ramp.png}
\caption{The palette ramp, paper on the left to heaviest ink on the right.
Lightness falls monotonically; hue sweeps $250^\circ$, an arc rather than the
circle so the ends stay apart.}
\end{figure}

\subsection{One pixel per transaction}

A colour is a distribution over block ids, so the obvious picture of one is
\emph{when} the coins came from and \emph{how concentrated} that was.
\texttt{oklch::tint} maps the mean block to hue over a $250^\circ$ arc and the
concentration, $1/(1+\text{spread}/45000)$, to chroma. A coinbase has zero
spread and comes out fully saturated; coins that have been through everything
come out grey. Mixing literally desaturates.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.47\textwidth]{figures/chain-sampled.png}
\hfill
\includegraphics[width=0.47\textwidth]{figures/chain-slice.png}
\caption{Left: the whole 2022 chain, $1024^2$ cells, one sampled transaction
each. Right: one million consecutive transactions at one pixel each, no binning.
Warm specks are young, concentrated coins; the grey field is thoroughly mixed
money. The horizontal streaks on the right are runs of consecutive transactions
sharing fresh ancestry.}
\end{figure}

The first attempt \emph{averaged} the transactions falling in a cell and
produced a smooth cyan-to-grey gradient that says nothing. The reason is
structural: the mean block climbs with the record index, so an averaged cell is
nearly a function of its own position. Sampling one real transaction per cell
keeps the variance. Both renders are honest; only one is informative.

\subsection{One byte per transaction}
\label{sec:byte}

The picture above is a function of two numbers, so it can be written as one.
\bin{tint} \flag{index} quantises the colour onto a 256-entry table --- 32 hues
by 8 concentrations --- and writes one byte a transaction. Over the chain that is
778\,614\,320 bytes against \flag{rgb}'s 2.3\,GB and \flag{hex}'s 14\,GB:
1.0000011 bytes a record, the header and the palette and the table of breaks in
the transaction-id run being 0.00011\% of it.

The split was measured rather than chosen. Hue error falls as $0.327/H$ and
concentration error as $0.075/M$ under $HM \le 256$; they are equal at
$H = 33.4$, which predicts a worst case of 0.0139 in quadrature. Over
60\,000\,000 records the $32\times8$ table measures 0.005328 mean and 0.014707
worst $\Delta E$ in Oklab against the continuous tint --- the smallest worst case
of the sixteen candidates, and a just-noticeable difference is about 0.02, so
nothing in the picture moves visibly. Quantising the spread rather than the
concentration, or one bin per doubling the way \bin{tx-view} does, is worse on
both statistics (0.007344 and 0.015578). $85\times3$ wins the mean and loses the
worst at 0.027193, which is past a JND; the worst is what was minimised, because
a mean hides exactly the transactions a picture of concentration is about.

A raw byte stream is not self-describing, and three things would have been lost
within the year: what the byte indexes, the axis constants it was quantised
against, and which record each byte is --- the moments file's first column is a
transaction id, and Section~\ref{sec:matrix} says why that is not the record
ordinal. So the file carries an 80-byte header with the axis constants, the
768-byte palette, and a table naming every place the id run breaks; over the
whole chain that table is four entries. The byte is derived from the moments
file and reproducible from it. It does not replace it.

\subsection{Every way it can be invoked}

The synopsis above is a grammar; this is the matrix. Twenty flags and two

hold byte for byte, which is the crate's usual way of making a claim

\paragraph{A short corpus needs \flag{blocks}, and cannot have it.} The band
width is \texttt{ceil(-{}-blocks / K)}, with \flag{blocks} defaulting to the
whole 2022 chain, $762\,261$; at $K=1024$ that is a band 745 blocks wide, so
every block of a short prefix falls in band~0 and the projection does nothing.
Over \texttt{synth2000}, which reaches block 285, \flag{bands}~1, 16, 64 and
1024 write byte-identical terms, md5 \texttt{4aa6ea15db9f}. Over
\texttt{real2000}, which reaches block 1\,969, that identity holds only to
$K=64$: at $K=1024$ the 745-wide bands split the blocks across bands 0, 1 and 2
and 1\,237 of the 2\,000 lines change. Nor is every line \texttt{1:0} where
the identity does hold --- the \texttt{f32} lanes drift, and 58 of
\texttt{synth2000}'s 2\,000 lines print \texttt{0.99999994:0} (45),
\texttt{1.0000001:0} (9) or \texttt{0.9999999:0} (4). The colour is computed
correctly and merely projected onto one band, and \flag{sum} and \flag{moments}
are unaffected because they read the carried moments --- but anyone measuring
band behaviour on a short prefix without \flag{blocks} is measuring nothing.
Passing \flag{blocks} is what aborts the run, which is
item~\ref{leak:abort} of \emph{Where the rule leaks} below.

Source, release policy, concurrency and instrumentation compose freely and with
everything else: \texttt{all -{}-bands 1024 -{}-moments -{}-matrix s.mat
-{}-release-oracle o.u32} and \texttt{2000 -{}-bands 1024 -{}-moments <

answers. The oracle takes \flag{weighted} \flag{sum} over 200\,000 synthetic
records from 9.16\,s to 5.91\,s and from 4\,684\,060--4\,689\,784\,KB resident
to 321\,980--328\,360\,KB; \flag{threads} takes 50\,000 weighted terms from
1.48\,s to 0.25\,s at eight threads, and 68\,992\,KB resident to
107\,392--120\,960\,KB. Both are the best of five runs on a 112-thread
machine at load average 19, and both are wall clock, so read them as ratios
rather than as constants. \flag{matrix} changes neither, and also retires the survey
pass for a picture, because the header states both dimensions.

Two edges of that group are worth naming. \flag{threads} is honoured for a
\flag{matrix} source only in the formatter pool: there is no reading thread,
because a matrix is decoded on the fold thread (Section~\ref{sec:matrix}'s last
open item). And \flag{release-oracle} is unusable without an explicit limit,
since the default is $1\,000\,001$ and the test is
$\text{oracle records} \ge \text{limit}$. A 2\,000-record oracle is refused on
a bare command line, accepted under \texttt{2000} and under \texttt{all}, and
accepted under \texttt{3000} only if \flag{rows} narrows the limit to 2\,000
or fewer: \texttt{3000 -{}-rows 0..2000 -{}-png p.png} is accepted,
\texttt{3000 -{}-rows 0..2001 -{}-png p.png} refused. The \flag{png} is not
decoration --- \flag{rows} is one of the three flags that need a sink to
describe.

\paragraph{What a pixel means, decoded rather than described.} Under
\flag{rings} and \flag{sets} a pixel is one bit, cleared where the block is in
the support --- the inked columns of the last row are exactly the exponents of
the last \flag{sets} line. Under \flag{weighted} it is eight bits,
$255 - \mathrm{round}(w^{1/2.2}\cdot 255)$: weight 0.778 gives sample 27, 0.0560
gives 186, 0.0165 gives 216, 0.00173 gives 241, paper is 255. \flag{palette}
writes those same bytes with colour type 3 and a 768-byte \texttt{PLTE} whose
entry 0 is $(86,29,50)$ and entry 255 $(250,250,250)$, so a smaller index is
heavier ink exactly as a smaller grey is. \flag{bin}~$n$ makes a row the union of
$n$ transactions, which under \flag{weighted} is the per-block \emph{maximum} of
their weights --- the darker pixel wins, which is the same rule as ``counted
once'' when every weight is 1. \flag{fold} shades a cell by the coverage of its
rectangle, so the same records come out genuinely lighter weighted than flat:
mean ink 1.25/255 against 6.83/255. \flag{gain}~4 lifts that to 2.35, which is
$1.25 \times 4^{1/2.2} = 1.25 \times 1.88$ and not $4\times$ anything --- the
gain is applied before the gamma, so the sample scales as $x^{1/2.2}$.

\flag{blocks} is a floor and not a crop, and it fails after the file exists:
\flag{png} \flag{blocks}~200 over \texttt{synth2000}, which reaches block 285,
prints ``2000 records over 286 blocks, so a 200 x 2000 picture'', then the
too-narrow refusal of Table~\ref{tab:refusals-form}, and leaves a 33-byte stub.
A larger value pads: the surveyed 286 columns write 7\,316 bytes and
\flag{blocks}~400 writes 7\,761. What is capped in \flag{fold}, alone among
the sinks, is the \emph{cell grid} --- never more than $1024\times1024$ cells
however many records there are, which is why it is the only picture a long run
can write. The file still grows with the run: \texttt{synth20k} gives
$1024\times1024$ cells of $3\times20$ pixels each and 114\,511 bytes,
\texttt{synth2000} $286\times1024$ cells of $1\times2$ and 10\,063.

\paragraph{The value syntax.} Every value-taking flag accepts
\texttt{-{}-name value} and \texttt{-{}-name=value}. A separate word is taken
only if it does not begin with \texttt{-{}-}, and a glued value must follow an
\texttt{=}, so \texttt{-{}-blocksy} is not \flag{blocks}, and a trailing
\texttt{-{}-png} with no path after it falls through to the positional parse
and prints the usage line rather than taking an empty path and carrying on.
That is deliberate: a flag that eats the next flag is the failure mode this
parser is shaped to avoid.

\paragraph{What is refused, and what it says.} Forty-three refusal sites in the
source collapse into the forty-two rows of Tables~\ref{tab:refusals-meaning},
\ref{tab:refusals-empty} and~\ref{tab:refusals-form} --- the picture
destinations clash through two arms of the same helper, so one message has two
sites. They are checked in four places: inside the argument loop, as each value
is parsed and as each backend and picture flag is chosen; after the whole
command line, for contradictions between two flags, so that neither order is
privileged and the message can name both; before the survey, for feature
absence, so the refusal comes ahead of a whole pass over 150\,GB; and before the
first record, for the release oracle, because a stale one produces a wrong
answer rather than a crash.

The place decides \emph{precedence} when two refusals are both due, and the
order is the reading order of the three places rather than anything about the
flags. The two-picture clash is raised by \texttt{choose} inside the argument
loop (\texttt{src/main.rs:1387--1399}, called at 1493 and 1508) and the
\flag{palette} clash with a non-raster sink after it, in \texttt{main}
(\texttt{1727--1745}); both therefore beat anything in \texttt{plan}. Inside
\texttt{plan} the feature checks (\texttt{1131--1142}) come before the
\flag{sum}-with-a-picture check that follows them, so the feature check wins
where the two collide: \texttt{500 -{}-sum -{}-pdf p.pdf} reports the missing
\texttt{pdf} feature and never mentions \flag{sum}, while
\texttt{500 -{}-sum -{}-fold p.png}, which needs no feature, reports the
contradiction. The consequence worth stating is that a message quoted from this
build is build-dependent: with \texttt{-{}-features pdf} the first of those two
command lines prints the other message. No row of the three tables is

\paragraph{Where the rule leaks.} Three accepted combinations do quietly
nothing, two do something other than what they read as, and one aborts. They
are kept here because the alternative is a section claiming a rule the binary
does not keep.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{\flag{moments} with a picture is accepted, and means
        \flag{weighted}.} \texttt{500 -{}-moments -{}-png p.png} over
        \texttt{real2000} is byte-identical to \texttt{500 -{}-weighted
        -{}-png p.png} (md5 \texttt{59934dd58d4f}) and different from
        \texttt{500 -{}-png p.png}, which is 1-bit flat ink
        (\texttt{da4114a1ee28}). The three numbers are dropped --- a picture
        has no lines --- and all that survives of the flag is that it selected
        a weighted backend. \flag{sum} in the same position is refused;
        \flag{moments} is simply never checked.
  \item \textbf{\flag{gain} with no picture at all is ignored, and not even
        validated.} \texttt{500 -{}-gain 2}, \texttt{-{}-gain 0},
        \texttt{-{}-gain -1} and \texttt{-{}-gain inf} all exit 0 with output
        byte-identical to \texttt{500}. Both gain refusals are real --- refused
        with \flag{png}, honoured with \flag{fold} --- but they live after the
        picture-less early return, which names only \flag{blocks}, \flag{bin}
        and \flag{rows}. It is the one place the flag is inert.
  \item \textbf{The backend contradictions are asymmetric.} The guard looks
        backwards at whether a backend was already chosen, so the same pair is
        refused in one order and silently obeyed in the other. Over
        \texttt{real2000}: \flag{rings} \flag{weighted} is refused,
        \flag{weighted} \flag{rings} gives rings (\texttt{c7d24fab0937});
        \flag{sets} \flag{bands}~64 is refused, \flag{bands}~64 \flag{sets}
        gives sets (the same md5); \flag{bands}~64 \flag{weighted} gives plain
        weighted with the bands dropped (\texttt{74952ce95eb0}), while
        \flag{weighted} \flag{bands}~64 gives bands (\texttt{154215bb3aaf}).
        Every backend flag is last-wins except the one guard that is not.
  \item \label{leak:blocks}\textbf{An overridden \flag{bands} still swallows \flag{blocks}.}
        \texttt{2000 -{}-bands 64 -{}-weighted -{}-blocks 12345} exits 0 with
        both flags doing nothing --- it is plain \flag{weighted} output ---
        because \flag{blocks} is \emph{taken} whenever \flag{bands} was named
        at all, which also lets it escape its own ``\flag{blocks} describes the
        picture'' refusal, the one it gets when passed alone.
  \item \textbf{\flag{matrix} catches records on a redirect and not on a pipe.}
        \texttt{-{}-matrix s.mat < records} is refused by name and byte count;
        \texttt{cat records | \ldots\ -{}-matrix s.mat} exits 0 and the piped
        records are read by nothing. The check measures what it can seek over,
        so the case a user is most likely to type at a 150\,GB file --- down a
        pipe --- is the case it cannot see.
  \item \label{leak:abort}\textbf{\flag{bands} with \flag{blocks} aborts instead of refusing.}
        \flag{bands}~64 \flag{blocks}~0 panics at \texttt{src/bands.rs:113},
        ``a band needs at least one block'' --- where \flag{blocks}~0 is refused
        politely everywhere else. \flag{bands}~64 \flag{blocks}~100 over
        \texttt{synth2000} panics at \texttt{src/bands.rs:123} with exit 134 and
        ``block 128 is past the 128 blocks the bands cover; pass \flag{blocks}''
        --- which is wrong about its own number, since 100 was asked for and the
        coverage rounds up to $64\lceil 100/64 \rceil = 128$. Nothing reaches
        stdout: the terms go through a 1\,MiB \texttt{BufWriter} the abort
        never flushes, and \flag{bands} over \texttt{synth2000} writes 8.70
        bytes a record, so the first flush is some 120\,500 records away. Partial
        output appears only once a run has filled that buffer before the
        aborting record. It is the same root as
        item~\ref{leak:blocks}: \flag{blocks} is taken
        away from the validation that would have caught it, and an assertion
        becomes the error handler. It is also
        exactly the flag a short corpus needs, so the one combination that makes
        \flag{bands} measurable on a prefix is the one that dies.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Worked examples}

\paragraph{The whole chain, summarised.} Two passes, the first reusable for any
later run over the same file:

\begin{lstlisting}
cargo run --release --example lastspend -- lastspend.u32 < finalBCUTXO_2022.scm

coloring-bt-transactions all --bands 1024 --moments --stats \
    --release-oracle lastspend.u32 < finalBCUTXO_2022.scm \
  | zstd -12 -T16 -o colors.moments.zst
\end{lstlisting}

Measured: $778\,613\,440$ records in 1:30:48 at 143\,k records/s, 35.1\,GiB
resident, 12.28\,GB out. The audit line reports zero live colours at the end ---
every one freed the moment nothing could read it again.

\paragraph{Colours from that.}

\begin{lstlisting}
zstd -dc colors.moments.zst | tint --hex > colors.hex
zstd -dc colors.moments.zst | tint --png chain.png --side 1024 \
    --records 778613440 --sample
zstd -dc colors.moments.zst | tint --index chain.tint --palette chain.plte
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{An exact prefix.} The largest exact weighted run that completes on a
503\,GB machine is around 5\,M records:

\begin{lstlisting}
coloring-bt-transactions 5000000 --weighted --threads 16 \
    --release-oracle lastspend.u32 < finalBCUTXO_2022.scm \
  | zstd -6 --long=27 -T32 -o colors-5M.zst
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{The chain as a matrix.} One pass writes it, a second proves it, and
every run after that reads 10.6\,GB instead of 150:

\begin{lstlisting}
cargo run --release --example matrix -- chain.foldmat \
    < finalBCUTXO_2022.scm
cargo run --release --example matrix -- --check chain.foldmat \
    < finalBCUTXO_2022.scm

coloring-bt-transactions all --bands 1024 --moments --stats \
    --release-oracle lastspend.u32 --matrix chain.foldmat < /dev/null \
  | zstd -12 -T16 -o colors.moments.zst
\end{lstlisting}

The two artefacts compose and answer different questions: the oracle says when a
colour may be dropped, the matrix says what the fold reads at all.

\section{Verification}
\label{sec:verification}

Three habits do most of the work.

\paragraph{Two implementations, one diff.} \flag{rings} and \flag{sets} share no
code and must produce byte-identical output. So must a threaded run and a serial
one, an oracle run and a plain one, a \flag{palette} picture's samples and a
greyscale one's, a run off the records and the same run off a matrix
(Section~\ref{sec:matrix}), and an x86-64 run and an aarch64 one. Each of those
is an equivalence between things that could have disagreed.

The strongest instance: the whole-chain \flag{moments} output was compared, all
$778\,613\,440$ lines, against a 79-line standalone tool carrying three
\texttt{f64} accumulators and sharing only the record reader. The first three
columns are byte-identical.

\paragraph{Two architectures, one diff.} The aarch64 kernels used to be written
and unexercised. They are now cross-built here and run under \texttt{qemu-aarch64}
--- \texttt{make cross-aarch64} does both --- and the crate's 305 tests pass on
that target, including the twelve that hold \bin{digit\_run} and
\bin{common\_prefix} against their scalar twins over every length, offset and
2\,000 random buffers. The end-to-end statement is stronger: 300\,000 real 2022
records through \flag{sets} give 5\,179\,435\,527 bytes that are byte-for-byte
the same on the two architectures, and \flag{weighted}, \flag{bands} and
\flag{moments} agree too. What that does \emph{not} say is anything about speed:
\texttt{qemu-user} models no cache and no issue width, so the 9.6$\times$ it costs
is a property of the emulator. The NEON path is correct here and untimed
anywhere.

\paragraph{Mutation.} A test that cannot fail is worse than no test. Several
here were caught by deliberately breaking the code they cover: an oracle test
corpus in which \emph{every} colour was one block, so a mutation returning a
spread of zero for everything passed; a palette test asserting every channel was
$\le 255$ of a \texttt{u8}; a gamut test that a mapping returning grey would have
survived.

\paragraph{Say what was measured.} Every number in this article names the run
that produced it. Two claims that survived an adversarial review only in outline
--- an attribution of resident memory to allocator fragmentation, and a
phase-by-phase breakdown of the fold that was internally inconsistent by 110\%
of the quantity it decomposed --- are deliberately absent.

\section{What is left}

All four items that stood here are closed. The aarch64 kernels are exercised
(Section~\ref{sec:verification}); the per-transaction byte is written
(Section~\ref{sec:byte}); $A$ is on disk (Section~\ref{sec:matrix}) at 10.6\,GB
rather than the 19.5 this section predicted, and exact where that number was not;
and the band vectors have been written.

That last one was the one item here that was a run and not a program, and it
cost more than it was told it would. \flag{bands} 1024 over the whole chain,
\flag{threads}~32, off the matrix and under the release oracle: 778\,613\,440
records in \textbf{5:00:18} at 43\,k records/s and 37.0\,GB resident, writing
8.67\,TiB that \texttt{zstd -6 -{}-long=27 -T32} took to 1.52\,TiB, 17.54\%.
Not the 0.94\,TB this section promised --- ten times that, for the reason
Section~\ref{sec:output} now gives. The audit line closes it: nought live
colours at the end, all reachable.

It was also, in passing, the first whole-chain solve made off a matrix, which
was the second item on this list. Five hours of fold on a 10.6\,GB file that
was not parsed once.

One number came out of it that no prefix could have shown. The lanes are
\texttt{f32}, and over 778 million records of accumulation the worst drift of a
colour's mass from one is $3.138\times10^{-5}$ --- against the
$1.02\times10^{-13}$ the exact \texttt{f64} fold holds
(Section~\ref{sec:rule}). Eight orders of magnitude, bought knowingly: a band
lane is half the memory and the whole reason \flag{bands} 1024 fits at all. It
is a bound on what a banded coefficient means, and it belongs beside the number
rather than in place of it.

What is left now:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Nothing has read the band vectors back.} 1.67\,TB was written
        and verified only by the fold that wrote it; the equivalences of
        Section~\ref{sec:matrix} stop at the 20-million-record prefix. A
        whole-chain \flag{sets} or \flag{weighted} pair would settle it and is
        102\,TB a side, so it will not be that pair.
  \item \textbf{aarch64 is correct and untimed.} \texttt{qemu-user} models no
        microarchitecture, so nothing here says what NEON is worth on silicon.
        The Mach-O half of \texttt{asm-check}'s pattern has also only ever been
        matched against compiler-emitted assembly, never a linked binary: the
        macOS cross-build stops in a C dependency, not in this crate.
  \item \textbf{\flag{threads} does not spread the matrix decode.} A matrix is
        decoded on the fold thread, because \bin{prefetch} parses text and a
        second definition of what a record is would be the wrong kind of saving.
        The \texttt{GROUPS} index exists for exactly that and is unused: 16 bytes
        per 4\,096 rows, so any group can be decoded without its predecessor.
  \item \textbf{The effective block count is still outside the family.} It is
        quadratic (Section~\ref{sec:projections}), so no bounded per-colour state
        folds it, and nothing here changes that.
\end{itemize}
